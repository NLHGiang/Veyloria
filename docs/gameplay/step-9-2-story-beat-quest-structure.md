# 9.2 — STORY BEAT → QUEST STRUCTURE

## Purpose

9.2 defines how a story beat becomes a playable quest or gameplay sequence.

The objective is not to force every story beat into a traditional quest.

Instead, the quest structure is a flexible container for:

> **Question → Situation → Action → Discovery → World Response → Consequence → New Question**

A Veyloria quest should feel like a piece of the world unfolding around the player rather than a checklist assigned by the game.

## 1. Quest Definition

A quest is:

> **A playable structure built around a meaningful question, situation or problem.**

It may involve investigation, exploration, relationships, interaction, world change, consequence, memory, contradiction or ordinary life.

> **Quest ≠ Task**

> **Quest ≠ Objective List**

## 2. Quest Genesis

Every major quest should originate from one or more of:

### A. Story Question
A question created directly by the narrative.

### B. World Problem
Something in the world requires attention.

### C. Character Need
A character needs something from the player.

### D. Player Discovery
The player notices something without being explicitly instructed.

## 3. Quest Core

Every quest should have a central:

> **Question**

Examples:

- What happened here?
- Who is telling the truth?
- Why does this place remember me?
- What did I promise this person?
- Why did the settlement change?
- What happens if I refuse?

A quest can answer its question while creating a larger one.

## 4. Quest Structure

Canonical structure:

> **QUESTION → SITUATION → OBJECTIVE → EXPLORATION / ACTION → DISCOVERY → COMPLICATION → CHOICE / ACTION → WORLD RESPONSE → CONSEQUENCE → NEW INFORMATION → NEW QUESTION**

Not every quest needs every layer.

The structure is a grammar, not a checklist.

## 5. Question

A strong question has understandable context, emotional relevance, uncertainty, potential evidence and potential action.

Weak:

> Find the missing item.

Stronger:

> Why did this person hide the item instead of simply losing it?

## 6. Situation

A situation should contain:

> **Context + Tension + Opportunity**

It explains why the player must act now.

## 7. Objective

Objectives provide clarity:

> **What can I do next?**

without necessarily revealing:

> **What is really happening?**

Good:

> Talk to the three residents who were present that night.

Too revealing:

> Discover that the protagonist caused the incident.

## 8. Objective Types

- Explore
- Observe
- Investigate
- Social
- Interact
- Compare
- Revisit
- Consequence
- Choice

## 9. Quest Activity Structure

A quest may combine:

> **Explore + Notice + Investigate + Social + Interact + Revisit**

The quest should become a chain of discovery rather than a chain of errands.

## 10. Discovery

Discovery changes player knowledge.

It can be:

- evidence;
- environmental information;
- NPC behavior;
- relationship information;
- new access;
- contradiction;
- memory;
- consequence.

Discovery should answer one question while potentially creating another.

## 11. Complication

A complication prevents direct Objective → Completion flow.

Examples:

- conflicting information;
- contradictory evidence;
- changed location;
- previous choice affecting access;
- NPC refusal;
- incomplete memory;
- another character remembering differently.

## 12. Choice

Choices may concern:

- trust;
- honesty;
- confrontation;
- patience;
- help;
- refusal;
- disclosure;
- secrecy;
- relationship priority;
- interpretation.

Avoid binary Good / Evil framing.

## 13. World Response

After significant action:

> **The world reacts.**

Possible responses:

- NPC behavior;
- dialogue;
- relationship;
- object;
- location;
- access;
- future quest;
- evidence;
- another character;
- future event.

## 14. Consequence

### Minor
Local and immediate.

### Significant
Persistent quest, relationship, location or opportunity effect.

### Major
Changes broader story or world state.

A quest does not need multiple story branches to create consequence.

## 15. Quest Completion

Completion may mean:

- discovering an answer;
- accepting uncertainty;
- changing a relationship;
- altering a location;
- creating a future consequence;
- gaining access;
- realizing a memory is wrong.

Strong pattern:

> **Resolution → Recontextualization → New Question**

## 16. Quest Rewards

Preferred rewards:

- understanding;
- knowledge;
- evidence;
- access;
- relationship;
- recognition;
- new interaction;
- new interpretation;
- world-state change.

Traditional rewards may exist but should not replace narrative significance.

> **Reward curiosity, not compliance.**

## 17. Quest Types

### Investigation Quest
> Question → Evidence → Contradiction → Interpretation

### Relationship Quest
> Need → Interaction → Choice → Relationship Change

### Exploration Quest
> Curiosity → Discovery → Environmental Evidence → New Question

### World Change Quest
> Problem → Action → State Change → Consequence

### Revisit Quest
> Old Place → New Context → New Evidence → Reinterpretation

### Memory Quest
> Familiarity → Contradiction → Investigation → Memory Conflict

### Consequence Quest
> Previous Action → World Reaction → Discovery → New Decision

### Ordinary-Life Quest
> Everyday Situation → Player Participation → Relationship / World Familiarity

## 18. Quest Grammar

> **Q → S → O → A → D → C → R → K → NQ**

Where:

- Q = Question
- S = Situation
- O = Objective
- A = Action
- D = Discovery
- C = Complication
- R = Response
- K = Knowledge
- NQ = New Question

Choices and consequences may branch from Action, but branching is optional.

## 19. Linear vs Branching

### Linear
Use when narrative progression is fixed but player expression matters.

### Branching
Use when choice materially changes relationship, access, information, future state or interpretation.

### Convergent Branching

Preferred where possible:

> **Different Player Actions → Different Consequences → Shared Narrative Destination**

## 20. Quest State Model

Possible states:

- Available
- Active
- Investigating
- Complicated
- Decision
- Resolved
- Consequence
- Remembered
- Recontextualized

Not every quest needs every state.

## 21. Quest Memory

Important quests should define what the world remembers.

Example:

> Player helped NPC A instead of NPC B.

Future memory may affect trust, dialogue, relationships and opportunities.

The quest becomes part of world history.

## 22. Quest Dependencies

A quest may depend on:

- previous knowledge;
- relationship;
- world state;
- location state;
- previous choice;
- discovered evidence;
- Act progression.

Prefer meaningful narrative gates over arbitrary level gates.

## 23. Quest Failure

Failure can cause:

- missed opportunity;
- damaged relationship;
- different evidence;
- altered world state;
- future suspicion;
- new investigation;
- alternate interpretation.

> **Quest failure does not necessarily mean reload.**

Failure can become story.

## 24. Quest Revisit

A completed quest may become relevant again because:

- new evidence appears;
- an NPC remembers;
- the location changes;
- the player learns something new;
- a contradiction is discovered;
- a later event recontextualizes the quest.

> **Past gameplay → Future meaning**

## 25. Quest Design Anti-Patterns

Avoid:

- checklist quests;
- waypoint quests;
- exposition quests;
- combat padding;
- fake choices;
- reward-first quests;
- memory collectathons;
- reset quests.

## 26. Quest Quality Test

Before approving a quest:

1. What is the central question?
2. Why does the player care?
3. What creates the situation?
4. What can the player actually do?
5. What does the player need to discover?
6. Which activities are involved?
7. Which mechanics are required?
8. What can the player choose?
9. How does the world respond?
10. What does the world remember?
11. What consequence persists?
12. What does the player understand afterward?
13. What new question is created?
14. Can the quest be revisited or recontextualized?
15. Does the quest feel specifically like Veyloria?

Every major quest should eventually be documented as:

## 28. Example — THE HOUSE THAT REMEMBERS

### Story Intent
Introduce the first major contradiction between player memory and world memory.

### Canonical Truth
The protagonist previously lived in the abandoned house.

### Player Knowledge
The player believes they have never lived there.

### Central Question
> **Why does this house feel familiar?**

### Situation
An NPC refuses to enter the house and reacts strangely when the protagonist approaches.

### Objective
Investigate why the NPC is afraid of the house.

### Activities
> Explore → Notice → Investigate → Social → Revisit

### Discovery
The player finds an object that appears to belong to them.

### Complication
The NPC claims the protagonist left the house years ago.

### Choice
The player may trust, challenge, hide the evidence or reveal it.

### World Response
The NPC remembers the player's attitude.

### Consequence
The NPC may become more or less willing to discuss the past later.

### New Information
The protagonist had a life in the settlement.

### Reinterpretation
> “I have never been here.”
>
> becomes:
>
> “I may have been here before.”

### New Question
> **Who was I before I forgot?**

### Progression
Understanding, not power.

### Revisit
Returning later may reveal changed objects, new dialogue or new evidence.

## 29. Quest Identity Principle

A Veyloria quest should leave the player with more than:

> **“I completed something.”**

It should leave:

> **“I discovered something.”**

or:

> **“I changed something.”**

or:

> **“Someone remembers what I did.”**

or:

> **“I now see something differently.”**

The strongest quests create all four.

## 30. 9.2 Completion Criterion

9.2 is complete when any mapped story beat can become a quest structure that:

- gives the player a clear immediate purpose;
- preserves narrative uncertainty;
- creates meaningful player action;
- uses mechanics intentionally;
- produces world response;
- leaves persistent traces;
- creates understanding;
- generates a new question.

Canonical quest grammar:

> **Question → Situation → Objective → Action → Discovery → Complication → Choice → World Response → Consequence → New Information → New Question**
